# 03. Content Quality & Information Architecture Audit

> A practical framework for evaluating content usefulness, originality, accuracy, topical coverage, information architecture, and the overall organization of website information.

**Audit category:** Content Quality & Information Architecture  
**Difficulty:** Beginner to Advanced  
**Applies to:** Business websites, blogs, ecommerce stores, publishers, SaaS platforms, educational websites, and service providers  
**Priority:** High

---

## 1. Purpose of This Audit

Content is one of the most important parts of a website's search visibility and user experience.

A technically accessible website may still struggle to attract qualified visitors if its content does not answer users' questions, explain its services, demonstrate expertise, or provide a clear path to the information they need.

This audit evaluates whether website content is:

- Useful and relevant to the intended audience
- Original and meaningfully differentiated
- Accurate, trustworthy, and up to date
- Aligned with user search intent
- Organized into a logical information architecture
- Easy to read, understand, and navigate
- Supported by appropriate evidence and references
- Connected through meaningful internal links
- Suitable for its intended business and conversion goals
- Understandable by search engines and other information retrieval systems

**Core principle:** Create content to help people accomplish something. Do not create pages merely to target keywords, increase word count, or fill a website with repetitive information.

Google's guidance emphasizes helpful, reliable, people-first content. There is no universal ideal word count or guaranteed content formula that automatically produces better rankings.

Reference: [Creating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)

## 2. Understand the Audience & Content Purpose

Before evaluating individual pages, identify who the content is intended to help.

### 2.1 Define the target audience

- [ ] Identify the primary audience.
- [ ] Identify secondary audience groups where relevant.
- [ ] Understand their knowledge level.
- [ ] Identify their problems, needs, and expectations.
- [ ] Understand what information they need before making a decision.
- [ ] Identify the questions they commonly ask.
- [ ] Consider language, location, and cultural context.
- [ ] Identify accessibility or usability requirements.

**Why it matters:** Content written for beginners may not satisfy experienced professionals. Similarly, a business buyer and a general consumer may need different information before taking action.

**How to check:**

Use:

- Customer interviews
- Support conversations
- Sales enquiries
- Website analytics
- Search Console queries
- Customer reviews
- Community discussions
- Search suggestions
- Surveys and feedback

Do not assume that every visitor has the same intent or level of knowledge.

### 2.2 Map the customer journey

Identify the information users need at different stages.

| Journey stage | User's need | Suitable content |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Understand a problem | Educational articles and explainers |
| Research | Explore possible solutions | Comparisons and detailed guides |
| Evaluation | Assess available options | Reviews, case studies, service details |
| Decision | Choose a provider or product | Pricing, demonstrations, FAQs, testimonials |
| After purchase | Use or maintain a solution | Tutorials, support documentation, troubleshooting |

**Audit checks:**

- [ ] Important customer questions are answered.
- [ ] Content supports relevant stages of the customer journey.
- [ ] Users can move naturally from learning to evaluating solutions.
- [ ] Important decision-making information is easy to find.
- [ ] Existing customers can access useful support information.
- [ ] Content does not push a sales message when users need an objective explanation.

### 2.3 Define the purpose of every important page

Every major page should have a clear primary purpose.

Examples:

- Explain a topic.
- Introduce a business.
- Describe a service.
- Compare products.
- Help users complete a task.
- Answer a specific question.
- Generate a qualified enquiry.
- Support an existing customer.

**Audit checks:**

- [ ] The primary purpose is clear.
- [ ] The page serves a defined audience.
- [ ] Its content matches the page type.
- [ ] Its main call to action is appropriate.
- [ ] The page adds value beyond repeating information available elsewhere.

**Fix:** Rewrite, consolidate, reposition, or remove pages that have no clear purpose or meaningful audience value.

## 3. Search Intent & Content Relevance

Search intent describes what a person is trying to accomplish when searching for something.

A page can contain relevant keywords but still fail to satisfy the user's actual need.

### 3.1 Identify the dominant search intent

Common search intent categories include:

| Intent | User objective | Example query |
|---|---|---|
| Informational | Learn something | What is technical SEO? |
| Navigational | Find a specific website or page | Google Search Console login |
| Commercial investigation | Compare available options | Best web hosting for WordPress |
| Transactional | Complete an action or purchase | Buy a domain name |

These categories are useful planning models, not rigid classifications. A query may contain more than one intent.

### 3.2 Analyze search results

For important target queries:

- [ ] Review the pages currently appearing in search results.
- [ ] Identify the type of content users are being shown.
- [ ] Review the dominant format: guides, product pages, videos, comparisons, or other formats.
- [ ] Identify common questions and subtopics.
- [ ] Assess whether the search intent is informational or commercial.
- [ ] Check whether local or regional results are relevant.
- [ ] Identify what information existing results fail to explain clearly.
- [ ] Avoid copying competitors' content structures without evaluating their usefulness.

**How to check:**

Use Google Search, Bing, Search Console, keyword research platforms, and direct audience research.

Search results can change by location, language, device, and time. Record the context of your research.

### 3.3 Match page content to search intent

- [ ] The page directly addresses the main query or user need.
- [ ] The introduction makes the page's purpose clear.
- [ ] The main answer is not unnecessarily delayed.
- [ ] Supporting details are relevant to the topic.
- [ ] The content format suits the user's task.
- [ ] The page does not promise something it fails to deliver.
- [ ] The call to action matches the visitor's likely next step.

**Example:**

A user searching for "how to install WordPress" likely needs a practical tutorial with clear steps, prerequisites, and troubleshooting guidance.

A sales page for hosting services alone may not satisfy that need.

### 3.4 Evaluate content relevance

- [ ] The content is relevant to the website's audience.
- [ ] The topic supports the website's overall purpose.
- [ ] Examples are appropriate for the intended readers.
- [ ] Supporting sections contribute to the main topic.
- [ ] Unrelated sections are removed or moved to more suitable pages.
- [ ] Content is not expanded with irrelevant paragraphs just to increase length.

## 4. Content Quality & People-First Evaluation

### 4.1 Evaluate usefulness

Ask:

- [ ] Does the page answer the user's main question?
- [ ] Does it provide enough detail to help the reader take action?
- [ ] Does it explain important concepts rather than simply naming them?
- [ ] Does it include useful examples, instructions, or evidence?
- [ ] Does it address likely follow-up questions?
- [ ] Does it help users avoid common mistakes?
- [ ] Does it provide information that is difficult to obtain from a superficial search?
- [ ] Does it offer a clear next step where appropriate?

A useful page does not need to be extremely long. It needs to provide the right information at the right level of detail.

### 4.2 Evaluate originality and information gain

- [ ] The page contains original explanations or meaningful analysis.
- [ ] Important claims are independently checked.
- [ ] The content adds value beyond repeating competing pages.
- [ ] Examples are relevant and specific.
- [ ] Original research, testing, observations, or experience is included where available.
- [ ] Unique insights are clearly explained.
- [ ] Competitor content is not copied or lightly rewritten.
- [ ] Any quotations or reused material are properly attributed.

**Information gain** is a useful content-planning concept: consider what a reader learns from your page that they would not easily obtain from a generic summary.

It is not a standalone Google ranking score or a guaranteed ranking formula.

### 4.3 Evaluate depth and completeness

- [ ] Essential aspects of the topic are covered.
- [ ] Important limitations are explained.
- [ ] Relevant exceptions are mentioned.
- [ ] Technical terminology is explained when necessary.
- [ ] Examples support complex explanations.
- [ ] The page avoids unnecessary repetition.
- [ ] Supporting sections are connected to the main subject.
- [ ] The reader can understand the topic without having to visit several unrelated pages.

Do not treat exhaustive coverage as a requirement for every page. A focused page can be more useful than a broad article that attempts to cover everything.

### 4.4 Check for content written primarily for search engines

Look for:

- Excessive keyword repetition
- Unnatural headings
- Repetitive paragraphs
- Pages targeting nearly identical keyword variations
- Unnecessary city or location pages
- Content created at scale without meaningful review
- Generic introductions that delay the answer
- Unsubstantiated claims
- Large sections added only to increase article length

**Fix:** Prioritize clarity, originality, usefulness, and the actual needs of the intended audience.

## 5. Experience, Expertise, Authority & Trust

Google uses the concept of E-E-A-T: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness.

E-E-A-T is a framework described in Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines. It is not a single ranking factor or a score that website owners can directly optimize.

Trust is particularly important, and the type of evidence needed depends on the subject.

### 5.1 Demonstrate first-hand experience where relevant

- [ ] Product reviews explain actual usage or testing where claimed.
- [ ] Case studies describe real projects.
- [ ] Tutorials reflect practical knowledge.
- [ ] Recommendations explain their reasoning.
- [ ] Limitations and disadvantages are acknowledged.
- [ ] Images and screenshots are genuine or clearly identified as illustrative.
- [ ] First-hand claims can be supported by evidence.

Do not claim personal testing, customer results, or professional experience that did not happen.

### 5.2 Demonstrate subject knowledge

- [ ] Content uses correct terminology.
- [ ] Technical explanations are accurate.
- [ ] Important recommendations include reasoning.
- [ ] Relevant standards and official documentation are referenced.
- [ ] Complex topics receive appropriate review.
- [ ] High-impact claims are checked before publication.

For medical, financial, legal, and other high-impact subjects, use appropriately qualified expertise and reliable primary sources.

### 5.3 Review author information

- [ ] Important articles have clear authorship where appropriate.
- [ ] Author biographies accurately describe relevant experience.
- [ ] Professional qualifications are genuine and verifiable.
- [ ] Editorial or expert review is disclosed when applicable.
- [ ] Author pages are accessible.
- [ ] Contributors are credited appropriately.
- [ ] Anonymous content has a legitimate reason where author identification matters.

Not every page requires an individual author biography. The appropriate level of attribution depends on the website and content type.

### 5.4 Review business transparency

- [ ] About page explains who operates the website.
- [ ] Contact information is available where appropriate.
- [ ] Business identity is consistent.
- [ ] Policies are accessible.
- [ ] Pricing and service claims are clear.
- [ ] Affiliate relationships and sponsored content are disclosed.
- [ ] Testimonials and reviews are genuine.
- [ ] Editorial standards are documented where relevant.

### 5.5 Evaluate source quality

- [ ] Important factual claims have reliable supporting sources.
- [ ] Primary sources are preferred when available.
- [ ] Statistics include context and dates.
- [ ] Research findings are not misrepresented.
- [ ] External links lead to relevant, trustworthy resources.
- [ ] Sources are reviewed periodically.
- [ ] Unverified claims are removed or qualified.

Reference: [Google's Creating Helpful Content Guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)

## 6. Content Accuracy, Freshness & Maintenance

### 6.1 Review factual accuracy

- [ ] Product details are correct.
- [ ] Service descriptions are current.
- [ ] Prices and offers are accurate.
- [ ] Technical instructions still work.
- [ ] Statistics and research claims are verified.
- [ ] Legal and regulatory information is current where relevant.
- [ ] External references remain accessible.
- [ ] Outdated screenshots and examples are replaced.

### 6.2 Identify outdated content

Look for:

- Expired promotions
- Discontinued products
- Old software instructions
- Outdated screenshots
- Broken links
- Old pricing
- Historical statistics presented as current
- Superseded recommendations
- Incorrect dates or year references
- Changes in policies or regulations

### 6.3 Update content meaningfully

- [ ] Update information when it has genuinely changed.
- [ ] Add new evidence when useful.
- [ ] Remove obsolete recommendations.
- [ ] Correct factual errors.
- [ ] Improve unclear explanations.
- [ ] Update internal links.
- [ ] Review structured data after major content changes.
- [ ] Record substantial editorial changes.

Do not change publication dates merely to make old content appear fresh.

### 6.4 Decide what to do with old content

| Situation | Recommended action |
|---|---|
| Useful and accurate | Keep |
| Valuable but outdated | Update |
| Overlapping with another page | Consolidate |
| Low-value but still useful to users | Improve or reposition |
| No longer relevant with a replacement | Redirect appropriately |
| Permanently obsolete without a replacement | Consider a genuine 404 or 410 |
| Incorrect or harmful | Correct or remove |

Do not delete pages only because they receive little traffic. Check their business value, backlinks, conversions, historical relevance, and usefulness first.

## 7. Information Architecture & Website Structure

Information architecture (IA) is the way website information is organized, grouped, labeled, and connected.

A good structure helps visitors find what they need and helps search systems understand the relationship between pages.

### 7.1 Review the main navigation

- [ ] Main navigation reflects the website's most important sections.
- [ ] Labels are understandable.
- [ ] Navigation items are not unnecessarily numerous.
- [ ] Important pages are easy to access.
- [ ] Dropdowns work correctly.
- [ ] Mobile navigation is usable.
- [ ] Navigation does not contain broken or redirected links.
- [ ] Users can identify where they are within the website.

Avoid using vague navigation labels when clearer wording is available.

### 7.2 Review page hierarchy

Example:

```text
Home
├── Services
│   ├── Web Design
│   ├── Web Development
│   └── SEO Services
├── Resources
│   ├── SEO Guides
│   ├── Hosting Guides
│   └── Tutorials
├── About
└── Contact
```

- [ ] Related pages are grouped logically.
- [ ] Parent and child relationships make sense.
- [ ] Important pages are not unnecessarily buried.
- [ ] Categories have a clear purpose.
- [ ] Navigation and breadcrumbs reflect the intended structure.
- [ ] The structure can accommodate future content without becoming confusing.

This is an illustrative structure, not a mandatory hierarchy for every website.

### 7.3 Review content categories and taxonomy

- [ ] Categories represent meaningful subject groups.
- [ ] Category names are descriptive.
- [ ] Categories are not excessively broad or narrow.
- [ ] Tags are used consistently where useful.
- [ ] Duplicate categories are consolidated.
- [ ] Empty or low-value archive pages are reviewed.
- [ ] Category pages provide value beyond listing links when appropriate.

Do not create dozens of nearly identical categories or tags without a clear user benefit.

### 7.4 Create a logical content hierarchy

A practical hierarchy might include:

- Main topic pages
- Supporting subtopic pages
- Detailed tutorials
- Reference resources
- Case studies
- Related service or product pages

The structure should reflect genuine relationships between subjects rather than being created solely to force a keyword hierarchy.

## 8. Topic Clusters & Topical Coverage

Topic clusters are a content planning approach for organizing related information around a broader subject.

They can help improve content organization and reduce unnecessary duplication. They are not a guaranteed Google ranking technique.

### 8.1 Identify main topic areas

- [ ] Identify the website's primary subject areas.
- [ ] Identify the most important topics for the audience.
- [ ] Separate core topics from supporting topics.
- [ ] Identify gaps in useful information.
- [ ] Identify subjects outside the website's expertise or purpose.
- [ ] Prioritize topics based on audience needs and business relevance.

### 8.2 Map pillar and supporting pages

Example:

```text
                    SEO Guide
                        |
        ┌───────────────┼───────────────┐
        |               |               |
   Technical SEO    On-Page SEO     Content SEO
        |               |               |
    Crawlability      Titles       Content Quality
    Indexing          Headings     Content Updates
    Sitemaps           URLs         Content Audits
```

- [ ] The main page provides a useful overview of its topic.
- [ ] Supporting pages explore distinct subtopics.
- [ ] Pages are not simply rewritten versions of each other.
- [ ] Internal links connect genuinely related content.
- [ ] Each page has a distinct purpose.
- [ ] Important supporting content is easy to discover.

### 8.3 Identify content gaps

Compare your existing coverage with:

- Audience questions
- Search queries
- Customer support questions
- Competitor content
- Industry developments
- Product or service information needs
- Relevant community discussions

A content gap is an opportunity only when the missing information is useful to the intended audience.

Do not publish a page just because a competitor has one.

### 8.4 Review topical overlap

- [ ] Multiple pages do not unnecessarily target the same intent.
- [ ] Similar articles have clearly differentiated purposes.
- [ ] Important topics have a clear primary page where appropriate.
- [ ] Supporting pages link to relevant main resources.
- [ ] Overlapping content is consolidated when useful.

Keyword cannibalization is often used to describe overlapping pages competing for similar searches. However, ranking fluctuations alone do not prove cannibalization. Review intent, query data, page relevance, and search results before changing URLs or merging content.

## 9. Content Format, Readability & Editorial Structure

### 9.1 Review page structure

- [ ] Each page has a clear title.
- [ ] The introduction explains what the reader will learn.
- [ ] Main sections follow a logical order.
- [ ] Headings describe the content beneath them.
- [ ] Paragraphs are reasonably easy to scan.
- [ ] Lists and tables are used when they genuinely improve understanding.
- [ ] Important information is not buried unnecessarily.
- [ ] Conclusions and next steps are useful.

Use H1, H2, and H3 headings to create a meaningful document structure. Do not treat the number of H1 headings as a universal ranking rule. Focus on clear page organization and accessible headings.

### 9.2 Review readability

- [ ] Sentences are understandable.
- [ ] Unnecessary jargon is avoided.
- [ ] Technical terms are explained.
- [ ] Paragraphs do not contain several unrelated ideas.
- [ ] Examples clarify difficult concepts.
- [ ] Tone suits the intended audience.
- [ ] Grammar and spelling are checked.
- [ ] Important instructions are unambiguous.

Simple language does not mean oversimplifying complex subjects. Explain difficult topics accurately and clearly.

### 9.3 Use media appropriately

- [ ] Images support the surrounding explanation.
- [ ] Diagrams clarify processes or relationships.
- [ ] Screenshots are current.
- [ ] Videos provide additional value.
- [ ] Tables improve comparison or understanding.
- [ ] Charts use accurate data and clear labels.
- [ ] Media does not distract from the main purpose.
- [ ] Copyright and licensing requirements are respected.

Do not add decorative images merely to increase page length.

### 9.4 Review calls to action

- [ ] The main call to action is clear.
- [ ] It matches the page's purpose.
- [ ] It is not excessively repetitive.
- [ ] Buttons use descriptive wording.
- [ ] Users understand what happens after clicking.
- [ ] Commercial recommendations are relevant.
- [ ] Affiliate or sponsored relationships are disclosed.

## 10. Content Duplication & Consolidation

Duplicate or highly similar content can make a website harder to maintain and may confuse users about which page is most useful.

### 10.1 Identify duplication

Check for:

- Identical pages on multiple URLs
- Near-identical blog posts
- Repeated service descriptions
- Multiple pages targeting the same intent
- Duplicate location pages
- Repeated product descriptions
- Automatically generated archive pages
- Similar landing pages with minimal differences

### 10.2 Choose the correct action

| Finding | Possible action |
|---|---|
| Exact duplicate URLs | Consolidate URL signals |
| Similar pages with different purposes | Clarify their scope |
| Multiple weak articles on one topic | Consider merging |
| Outdated page with a strong replacement | Redirect if appropriate |
| Similar products with genuinely different details | Keep separate |
| Repeated boilerplate text | Improve page-specific information |

Do not assume that every instance of similar text is harmful. Product descriptions, legal notices, navigation, and other repeated elements can be legitimate.

### 10.3 Preserve content value during consolidation

- [ ] Identify the strongest existing page.
- [ ] Review traffic, backlinks, conversions, and historical value.
- [ ] Preserve useful information from supporting pages.
- [ ] Select the most appropriate destination URL.
- [ ] Implement relevant redirects where needed.
- [ ] Update internal links.
- [ ] Update sitemap and canonical signals.
- [ ] Monitor the affected URLs after deployment.

## 11. Content Governance & Editorial Workflow

A content strategy needs a process for creating, reviewing, publishing, and maintaining information.

### 11.1 Define editorial responsibilities

- [ ] Assign content ownership.
- [ ] Define who researches topics.
- [ ] Define who writes and edits.
- [ ] Identify who reviews technical or specialist claims.
- [ ] Establish publication approval.
- [ ] Maintain a correction process.
- [ ] Define responsibility for ongoing updates.

### 11.2 Create a content brief

A useful content brief may contain:

| Field | Details |
|---|---|
| Topic | Main subject |
| Target audience | Intended readers |
| Primary intent | What the reader wants |
| Primary query | Main search query where relevant |
| Supporting questions | Related information needs |
| Page purpose | Education, service, comparison, etc. |
| Key information | Essential topics |
| Sources | Reliable references |
| Differentiation | What additional value the page provides |
| Internal links | Relevant existing pages |
| CTA | Intended next action |
| Reviewer | Responsible person |

Do not treat keyword volume as the only reason to create content.

### 11.3 Maintain a content calendar

- [ ] Record planned content.
- [ ] Assign owners and deadlines.
- [ ] Track content status.
- [ ] Schedule reviews for time-sensitive pages.
- [ ] Prioritize updates to important existing content.
- [ ] Avoid publishing more content than the team can maintain.

### 11.4 Maintain editorial standards

- [ ] Define tone and writing style.
- [ ] Define sourcing requirements.
- [ ] Establish fact-checking expectations.
- [ ] Define image and media standards.
- [ ] Establish disclosure requirements.
- [ ] Define AI-assisted content review rules.
- [ ] Maintain version history for significant changes.

## 12. AI-Assisted Content Quality & Editorial Review

AI tools can help with research, outlines, editing, summarization, and idea generation. They can also introduce errors, generic explanations, fabricated references, and repetitive content.

The goal is not to avoid AI entirely. The goal is to maintain editorial responsibility and genuine value.

### 12.1 Review AI-assisted content

- [ ] Factual claims are independently verified.
- [ ] References and citations actually support the claims.
- [ ] Statistics and quotations are checked.
- [ ] Product and service information is accurate.
- [ ] Examples are relevant to the audience.
- [ ] Content is not a generic rewrite of existing pages.
- [ ] The final article has a clear purpose.
- [ ] A responsible editor reviews important content.
- [ ] Sensitive or high-impact topics receive appropriate expert review.

### 12.2 Avoid low-value mass production

Watch for:

- Large volumes of similar pages
- Automated location pages with little local value
- Repetitive articles created from keyword variations
- Unverified AI-generated statistics
- Fabricated personal experiences
- Invented customer testimonials
- Fake expert biographies
- Nonexistent citations
- Pages published without meaningful editorial review

Using AI is not automatically a violation of search guidelines. The quality, purpose, originality, and usefulness of the resulting content matter.

### 12.3 Establish an AI editorial policy

Document:

- Which AI tools may be used
- Which tasks may be assisted by AI
- Which claims require independent verification
- Who is accountable for final publication
- How original research and experience are documented
- How copyright and privacy are handled
- Which topics require specialist review

Reference: [Google Guidance on AI-Generated Content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content)

## 13. Content Accessibility & Inclusive Communication

Content should be usable by people with different devices, abilities, and reading needs.

### 13.1 Review text accessibility

- [ ] Text has sufficient contrast.
- [ ] Font sizes are readable.
- [ ] Headings follow a meaningful structure.
- [ ] Links are distinguishable from ordinary text.
- [ ] Instructions do not rely only on colour.
- [ ] Important information is available as text rather than only inside images.
- [ ] Language is clear and understandable.

### 13.2 Review image and media accessibility

- [ ] Informative images have meaningful alternative text.
- [ ] Decorative images use appropriate empty alt attributes.
- [ ] Videos provide captions where needed.
- [ ] Audio content has suitable text alternatives.
- [ ] Charts include accessible explanations or data where practical.
- [ ] Important instructions are not available only through visual cues.

### 13.3 Review document and navigation accessibility

- [ ] Links have descriptive labels.
- [ ] Tables use appropriate headers.
- [ ] Interactive content is usable with a keyboard where applicable.
- [ ] Forms have understandable labels and error messages.
- [ ] Downloadable documents are accessible where practical.

Reference: [W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/)

Different website types need different content checks. Apply only the sections relevant to the website being audited.

- [ ] Process and deliverables are explained.

- [ ] Genuine testimonials or case studies support claims.
