# 06. AI Search Visibility Audit: GEO, AEO & LLMO

AI-powered search is changing how people discover information, compare products, evaluate businesses, and find answers online.

Traditional search engines primarily present lists of results, while AI-assisted experiences can summarize information, answer questions, compare sources, and cite selected websites.

This audit evaluates whether a website's content is discoverable, accessible, understandable, reliable, and useful across AI-assisted search and answer experiences.

It covers:

- **GEO (Generative Engine Optimization):** Improving the usefulness and discoverability of content in generative search experiences.
- **AEO (Answer Engine Optimization):** Structuring useful information so that it can satisfy direct questions and answer-oriented searches.
- **LLMO (Large Language Model Optimization):** Improving the clarity, reliability, and contextual usefulness of website information for systems powered by large language models.

These terms overlap considerably and do not represent three independent ranking systems. Their definitions are not universally standardized.

> **Important:** There is no guaranteed GEO, AEO, or LLMO formula that makes a website appear in AI-generated answers. Search engines and AI platforms use different systems, sources, models, and retrieval methods. This audit focuses on practical improvements rather than unsupported ranking promises.

---

## 1. Understand the AI Search Ecosystem

Before optimizing a website, identify the types of discovery experiences that matter to its audience.

### 1.1 Identify relevant AI search platforms

Review relevant experiences, which may include:

- Google AI Overviews
- Google AI Mode
- Microsoft Copilot
- ChatGPT Search
- Perplexity
- Other AI-powered search and answer platforms relevant to the target audience

Different platforms may use different search indexes, crawlers, retrieval systems, and citation methods.

### 1.2 Understand the main discovery models

| Discovery model | How it works |
|---|---|
| Traditional search | Presents ranked web results for a query |
| Answer engine | Provides direct answers to specific questions |
| Generative search | Generates responses using retrieved information and model knowledge |
| Conversational search | Allows users to ask follow-up questions and refine their intent |
| Agent-assisted discovery | May combine information retrieval with actions such as comparing, navigating, or completing tasks |

A platform may combine several of these models.

### 1.3 Define the website's AI visibility objectives

- [ ] Identify the platforms relevant to the target audience.
- [ ] Identify the topics for which the brand wants to be discovered.
- [ ] Identify important customer questions.
- [ ] Identify relevant products, services, and business entities.
- [ ] Define what a useful AI referral or citation would look like.
- [ ] Establish a baseline before implementing changes.

**Example:**

A hosting website may want to be discovered for questions such as:

- Which hosting is suitable for a beginner?
- What should I check before buying web hosting?
- How do shared and cloud hosting differ?
- What are the renewal costs of a hosting plan?

A local service business may instead focus on service availability, location, pricing, qualifications, and customer experience.

## 2. GEO, AEO & LLMO: Practical Differences

The terminology overlaps, but each label emphasizes a different perspective.

### 2.1 Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)

GEO focuses on making content useful and discoverable in generative search experiences.

Audit whether:

- [ ] Content explains topics accurately and comprehensively.
- [ ] Important facts are supported by reliable evidence.
- [ ] Original insights and examples are available.
- [ ] Content addresses complex questions and comparisons.
- [ ] Important information is accessible in crawlable formats.
- [ ] The page provides context that can be understood independently.
- [ ] The content offers meaningful value beyond generic summaries.

### 2.2 Answer Engine Optimization (AEO)

AEO focuses on satisfying questions through clear, useful, well-organized information.

Audit whether:

- [ ] Important customer questions are identified.
- [ ] Direct answers are provided where appropriate.
- [ ] Supporting explanations follow the answer.
- [ ] Instructions are presented in logical steps.
- [ ] Comparisons include clear evaluation criteria.
- [ ] Definitions explain technical terminology.
- [ ] Answers include limitations and relevant context.

### 2.3 Large Language Model Optimization (LLMO)

LLMO focuses on making website information clear, consistent, reliable, and contextually understandable for LLM-powered experiences.

Audit whether:

- [ ] Brand identity is clearly represented.
- [ ] Products and services have consistent descriptions.
- [ ] Important claims have identifiable sources.
- [ ] Author and organization information is transparent.
- [ ] Pages explain relationships between relevant entities.
- [ ] Content avoids ambiguity and contradictory statements.
- [ ] Information is available in formats that can be accessed and interpreted.

### 2.4 Apply one integrated optimization framework

Avoid creating three separate content strategies for the same page.

A useful page should:

1. Satisfy the user's actual need.
2. Explain the subject clearly.
3. Provide direct answers where appropriate.
4. Support important claims with evidence.
5. Include useful original information.
6. Be technically accessible.
7. Be easy to navigate and verify.

These practices support multiple discovery experiences without depending on a specific AI platform.

## 3. Search Intent & Conversational Query Research

People often use longer, more specific, and conversational questions when interacting with AI systems.

### 3.1 Identify real user questions

- [ ] Review Search Console queries.
- [ ] Review customer support questions.
- [ ] Review sales and enquiry conversations.
- [ ] Analyze relevant community discussions.
- [ ] Review product and service feedback.
- [ ] Identify recurring comparison questions.
- [ ] Identify questions that require multiple sources or steps.

Examples:

Traditional query:

`best web hosting`

Conversational query:

`Which web hosting should I choose for a small business website with limited technical knowledge?`

Follow-up questions may include:

- What will it cost after the introductory period?
- Is email hosting included?
- Can I upgrade later?
- What happens if my website receives more traffic?

### 3.2 Build a question and intent map

Group questions by intent.

| Intent | Example question |
|---|---|
| Informational | What is cloud hosting? |
| Comparison | Shared hosting vs cloud hosting: what is the difference? |
| Commercial | Which hosting provider is suitable for a small business? |
| Transactional | Where can I purchase a hosting plan? |
| Troubleshooting | Why is my WordPress website loading slowly? |
| Local | Which website development agency serves my area? |

### 3.3 Prioritize important questions

- [ ] Questions are relevant to the website's subject.
- [ ] Questions represent genuine audience needs.
- [ ] High-value questions receive appropriate coverage.
- [ ] Similar questions are grouped logically.
- [ ] Content is not expanded with irrelevant questions merely to increase page length.

**Deliverable:** A question map linking user questions to existing pages, missing content, and potential new resources.

## 4. Content Structure & Answer Extraction

A page should communicate its main information clearly, whether a person reads the entire article or scans a specific section.

### 4.1 Review direct answers

- [ ] Important questions receive clear answers.
- [ ] The answer appears near the relevant question or heading.
- [ ] Supporting explanation provides sufficient context.
- [ ] Important conditions and limitations are included.
- [ ] Answers avoid unsupported absolute claims.
- [ ] Content is not written as disconnected fragments.

**Example:**

Question: What is shared hosting?

Useful answer:

"Shared hosting is a web hosting arrangement in which multiple websites use resources from the same server. It is often suitable for smaller websites with moderate resource requirements, although performance and available resources depend on the provider and plan."

This gives a direct explanation and relevant context without pretending every shared hosting plan performs identically.

### 4.2 Improve content organization

- [ ] Pages have descriptive headings.
- [ ] Related information is grouped together.
- [ ] Definitions appear near the terminology they explain.
- [ ] Tables are used for genuine comparisons.
- [ ] Lists are used for procedures and requirements.
- [ ] Complex topics include practical examples.
- [ ] Important conclusions are supported by the preceding explanation.

### 4.3 Provide complete answers

A useful answer should often cover:

- What the subject is.
- How it works.
- Why it matters.
- When it is useful.
- What its limitations are.
- What alternatives exist.
- Where the reader can verify additional information.

Not every page needs all seven elements. Include what the user's question requires.

### 4.4 Avoid artificial answer formatting

- [ ] FAQs are included only when they add value.
- [ ] Questions are not repeated simply to target variations.
- [ ] Paragraphs remain natural and readable.
- [ ] Important information is not hidden behind unnecessary interactions.
- [ ] Content is not reduced to short, context-free answers.
- [ ] Keyword variations are not inserted unnaturally.

**Important:** There is no established requirement to use a particular paragraph length, question format, or answer word count to qualify for AI citations.

## 5. Original Information & Information Gain

Generic content that repeats existing information without adding value gives users little reason to prefer one source over another.

### 5.1 Evaluate content originality

- [ ] The page offers useful information beyond generic definitions.
- [ ] Original research is included where possible.
- [ ] Real-world examples support explanations.
- [ ] First-hand observations are accurately identified.
- [ ] Comparisons use transparent criteria.
- [ ] Case studies contain genuine evidence.
- [ ] Limitations and unexpected findings are discussed.

### 5.2 Review first-hand evidence

Where applicable, include:

- Product testing methods.
- Actual usage experience.
- Screenshots of genuine results.
- Documented project examples.
- Original measurements.
- Customer feedback with appropriate permission.
- Practical troubleshooting experience.

**Example:**

A hosting review becomes more useful when it explains the actual plan used, duration of usage, test conditions, observed limitations, renewal pricing, and support experience.

A generic list of hosting features provides less decision-making value.

### 5.3 Avoid fabricated evidence

- [ ] Statistics are traceable to their sources.
- [ ] Screenshots represent genuine results.
- [ ] Testimonials are authentic.
- [ ] Test results include relevant context.
- [ ] Personal experience is not invented.
- [ ] AI-generated claims are independently verified.

## 6. Entity Clarity & Knowledge Representation

An entity is a clearly identifiable person, organization, product, place, service, or concept.

Clear entity information helps people and information systems distinguish between similar names and understand relationships.

### 6.1 Review organization identity

- [ ] The organization has a consistent public name.
- [ ] Its website clearly explains what it does.
- [ ] Important business details are accurate.
- [ ] Official contact information is available.
- [ ] Relevant social and professional profiles are consistent.
- [ ] Brand descriptions do not contradict one another.

### 6.2 Review product and service entities

- [ ] Product names are consistent.
- [ ] Service descriptions are specific.
- [ ] Pricing and availability are accurate where published.
- [ ] Related products and services are distinguishable.
- [ ] Product specifications are available in readable text.
- [ ] Important relationships are explained.

### 6.3 Review people and author entities

- [ ] Authors have identifiable profiles where appropriate.
- [ ] Biographies are factual.
- [ ] Professional roles are represented accurately.
- [ ] Relevant qualifications and experience are documented.
- [ ] Author and organization relationships are clear.

### 6.4 Review structured entity information

Where appropriate:

- [ ] Organization structured data is accurate.
- [ ] Person structured data is accurate.
- [ ] Product structured data matches visible content.
- [ ] LocalBusiness information is correct where applicable.
- [ ] Breadcrumb relationships are consistent.
- [ ] Identifiers such as `sameAs` point to genuine official profiles.

Structured data can help describe entities, but it does not guarantee inclusion in AI responses or knowledge panels.

## 7. Sources, Citations & Information Reliability

AI-generated answers may rely on multiple sources. Websites should make important information easy to verify.

### 7.1 Review source quality

- [ ] Important factual claims have reliable supporting sources.
- [ ] Primary sources are preferred where available.
- [ ] Statistics include dates and relevant context.
- [ ] Research findings are represented accurately.
- [ ] Technical claims reference appropriate documentation.
- [ ] Sources are not presented out of context.
- [ ] Outdated references are replaced when necessary.

### 7.2 Improve citation clarity

- [ ] Sources are linked close to the claims they support.
- [ ] Source titles and publishers are identifiable.
- [ ] Important quotations are attributed correctly.
- [ ] Research methodology is explained where relevant.
- [ ] Data limitations are disclosed.
- [ ] Readers can independently verify important claims.

### 7.3 Distinguish facts, estimates, and opinions

- [ ] Verified facts are distinguishable from opinions.
- [ ] Estimates are identified as estimates.
- [ ] Predictions are not presented as established facts.
- [ ] Commercial recommendations disclose relevant relationships.
- [ ] Uncertainty is explained honestly.

### 7.4 Review information freshness

- [ ] Time-sensitive information is checked regularly.
- [ ] Product pricing and specifications are updated.
- [ ] Regulatory information is reviewed.
- [ ] Broken source links are fixed.
- [ ] Outdated recommendations are corrected.
- [ ] Update dates reflect meaningful changes.

## 8. Technical Accessibility for AI Crawlers

AI-related visibility depends partly on whether relevant systems can access the website. Different platforms use different crawlers and access mechanisms.

### 8.1 Review traditional search accessibility

- [ ] Important pages are crawlable.
- [ ] Important pages can be indexed where intended.
- [ ] Internal links make important pages discoverable.
- [ ] Content is available in a readable format.
- [ ] Canonical URLs are correct.
- [ ] Important pages do not accidentally use `noindex`.
- [ ] Server and CDN rules do not unintentionally block required crawlers.

See the Technical SEO & Crawlability audit for detailed indexing, robots, rendering, and sitemap checks.

### 8.2 Understand AI crawler identities

Different AI-related crawlers may have different purposes.

Examples include:

| Crawler | Associated purpose |
|---|---|
| OAI-SearchBot | Discovering and surfacing content for ChatGPT Search |
| GPTBot | Crawling content that may be used for OpenAI model training |
| ChatGPT-User | Fetching pages in response to certain user-initiated actions |
| Googlebot | Crawling Google Search content |
| Google-Extended | A Google-controlled token for certain AI training and grounding controls |
| Bingbot | Crawling content for Bing search and related experiences |
| PerplexityBot | Crawling content for Perplexity-related discovery |

Crawler names and policies can change. Always check the platform's current official documentation before changing access rules.

### 8.3 Review robots.txt policies

- [ ] Review which crawlers are allowed or blocked.
- [ ] Confirm rules are intentional.
- [ ] Check whether CDN or security systems apply additional restrictions.
- [ ] Investigate unexpected crawler access failures.
- [ ] Avoid blocking all AI crawlers without understanding the business implications.
- [ ] Document crawler policy decisions.

**Example robots.txt entries:**

```text
User-agent: OAI-SearchBot
Allow: /

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /
```

This example illustrates separate crawler policies. It is not a universal recommendation. Choose access rules according to the website owner's preferences and the platform's documented behavior.

**Important:** Allowing an AI crawler does not guarantee that the content will be cited or recommended. Blocking a training crawler does not necessarily block a separate search crawler.

### 8.4 Review content access

- [ ] Important content is available without mandatory login where public discovery is intended.
- [ ] Main information is not exclusively available through inaccessible scripts.
- [ ] Pages do not return unexpected access-denied responses.
- [ ] Bot protection does not unintentionally block legitimate crawlers.
- [ ] Important content is not hidden behind unnecessary interaction requirements.
- [ ] Server responses are checked for relevant user agents where practical.

### 8.5 Review content preview controls

Google supports controls such as:

- `nosnippet`
- `max-snippet`
- `data-nosnippet`
- `noindex`

These controls can affect how content is used or displayed in Google Search, including AI features.

- [ ] Existing snippet restrictions are intentional.
- [ ] Sensitive sections are protected where appropriate.
- [ ] Page-level restrictions are tested.
- [ ] The website owner understands the visibility trade-offs.
- [ ] Changes are verified after recrawling.

Do not add restrictive directives casually. They can reduce the content available for search snippets and some AI search experiences.

## 9. Structured Data & Machine-Readable Content

Structured data can provide additional context about information on a page.

However, there is no special schema type that guarantees AI search visibility.

### 9.1 Review relevant schema types

Depending on the page, consider:

- Organization
- Person
- Article
- Product
- LocalBusiness
- BreadcrumbList
- Event
- Recipe
- VideoObject

Only use schema types that accurately describe the visible content and meet the relevant platform guidelines.

### 9.2 Validate schema implementation

- [ ] Structured data matches visible page information.
- [ ] Important properties contain accurate values.
- [ ] Required fields are present where applicable.
- [ ] Duplicate or conflicting entities are investigated.
- [ ] Schema is validated after major template changes.
- [ ] Unsupported or misleading markup is removed.

Tools:

- [Google Rich Results Test](https://search.google.com/test/rich-results)
- [Schema.org Validator](https://validator.schema.org/)

### 9.3 Review machine-readable content quality

- [ ] Important information is expressed in clear text.
- [ ] Product specifications are not available only inside images.
- [ ] Tables contain meaningful labels.
- [ ] Units, dates, and currency are clearly identified.
- [ ] Data definitions are provided where needed.
- [ ] Relationships between entities are explained.
- [ ] Important downloadable resources have accessible descriptions.

### 9.4 Review AI-specific files and markup claims

- [ ] Evaluate proposed AI-specific files based on documented platform support.
- [ ] Do not assume an `llms.txt` file is required for search visibility.
- [ ] Do not treat custom AI schema as a guaranteed ranking method.
- [ ] Avoid adding unverified directives copied from marketing articles.
- [ ] Confirm whether a platform actually documents support for a proposed feature.

A file such as `llms.txt` may be used by some projects as an informational resource, but it is not a universal requirement for Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT Search, or other AI search experiences.

## 11. AI Search Visibility Measurement

AI visibility is difficult to measure consistently because responses can vary by query, location, platform, model, and time.

A good measurement system combines platform reporting, website analytics, and repeatable manual observations.

### 11.1 Establish a baseline

Record:

- Important target questions.
- Relevant AI platforms.
- Existing brand mentions.
- Existing citation examples.
- Website organic traffic.
- Referral traffic from AI platforms where identifiable.
- Important landing-page conversions.
- Current content and technical limitations.

### 11.2 Use available platform reporting

Useful sources include:

- Google Search Console.
- Bing Webmaster Tools.
- Website analytics platforms.
- Server logs.
- Referral reports.
- Available platform-specific AI performance reports.

**Google Search Console:** Google's documented AI features are included in the overall Search Console Performance reporting under the Web search type. They are not necessarily presented as a separate AI traffic channel.

**Bing Webmaster Tools:** Its AI Performance report provides citation-related insights for supported AI experiences, including cited pages and grounding queries.

### 11.3 Track meaningful metrics

| Metric | What it helps measure |
|---|---|
| AI citations | Visible references to website pages in supported AI answers |
| Cited URLs | Which pages are referenced |
| AI referral sessions | Visits arriving from identifiable AI platforms |
| Engaged sessions | Quality of visits from AI referrals |
| Conversions | Business outcomes from those visits |
| Branded search impressions | Changes in brand-related search interest |
| Qualified enquiries | Relevant leads generated |
| Citation accuracy | Whether references represent the content correctly |

### 11.4 Understand measurement limitations

- [ ] Citation counts are not treated as rankings.
- [ ] Citations are not confused with clicks.
- [ ] Referral traffic is not assumed to represent all AI exposure.
- [ ] Manual tests use consistent queries.
- [ ] Platform and date are recorded.
- [ ] Changes in models and search experiences are considered.
- [ ] Conversion quality is evaluated alongside visibility.

No single metric provides a complete measurement of AI search performance.

## 12. Manual AI Search Testing Framework

Use a repeatable testing process instead of relying on occasional random prompts.

### 12.1 Create a query set

Include several query types:

| Query type | Example |
|---|---|
| Definition | What is managed WordPress hosting? |
| Comparison | Managed hosting vs shared hosting |
| Recommendation | Which hosting is suitable for a small business? |
| Problem-solving | How can I reduce WordPress loading time? |
| Brand-specific | What services does [Brand Name] provide? |
| Product-specific | What are the limitations of [Product Name]? |
| Local discovery | Which website development agencies serve [Location]? |

### 12.2 Test consistently

For each query:

1. Use the same wording across testing sessions.
2. Record the platform and date.
3. Record the location or language where relevant.
4. Note whether the website is mentioned or cited.
5. Record the exact URL when available.
6. Check whether the information is accurate.
7. Record competing sources.
8. Repeat periodically.

### 12.3 Avoid misleading conclusions

- A single citation does not prove long-term visibility.
- A missing citation does not prove the website is technically blocked.
- Different answers may be generated for similar questions.
- Platform responses may change after model or retrieval updates.
- Personalization and location can influence results.

Treat manual testing as directional evidence, not a guaranteed ranking measurement.

## 15. Common GEO, AEO & LLMO Myths

Avoid treating unsupported marketing claims as established optimization practices.

| Myth | More accurate understanding |
|---|---|
| Every website needs `llms.txt` | No universal requirement is documented for major search visibility |
| AI systems only cite websites with special schema | No special schema guarantees citations |
| More FAQs automatically improve AI visibility | FAQs help only when they answer genuine user questions |
| Short answers always get cited | Answer usefulness depends on context, evidence, relevance, and platform behavior |
| Keyword density improves LLM visibility | There is no universal density formula |
| AI crawlers must all be allowed | Access decisions depend on the owner's objectives and platform policies |
| More backlinks guarantee AI recommendations | Backlinks do not guarantee AI mentions or citations |
| E-E-A-T is an AI ranking score | E-E-A-T is a quality assessment framework, not a public score |
| One successful AI citation proves optimization worked | A single observation is not sufficient evidence |
| AI-generated content automatically ranks | Content quality, usefulness, originality, and policy compliance matter |

## 16. Prioritization Framework

| Priority | Typical examples |
|---|---|
| Critical | Important content is accidentally blocked, business information is seriously misleading, or major technical access problems exist |
| High | Important pages lack essential information, contain significant factual errors, or are inaccessible to relevant search crawlers |
| Medium | Weak answer structure, missing source context, inconsistent entity descriptions, or outdated supporting content |
| Low | Optional content formatting improvements, additional examples, or experimental AI visibility tracking |

Prioritize using:

- Business impact.
- Audience relevance.
- Technical accessibility.
- Evidence of an actual problem.
- Implementation effort.
- Risk of unintended visibility restrictions.

Do not prioritize changes simply because a marketing article labels them as a GEO ranking factor.
